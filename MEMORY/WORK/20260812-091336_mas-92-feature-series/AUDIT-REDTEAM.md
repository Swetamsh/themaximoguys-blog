## CRITIC 1 — Skeptical IBM Maximo Field Architect

*Fifteen years on MAS and Maximo before it. Not an IBM shill — I have told customers to skip releases. But I have also watched a well-written blog post become the reason a customer sat on 8.11 for two extra years, and this post reads like that post.*

I will concede the genuinely good parts before I start. The `What We Could Not Verify` table is better practice than most vendor-adjacent content manages. The Db2 12 licence item is real and under-communicated. The closing observation that identity consolidation matters more than agentic AI is correct and I have said it myself. Now the problems.

## Synthesis

### Genuinely fair — these should change the post

Ranked by how much the post improves per unit of effort.

**1. Gate 7 is a changelog, not a cost model — and the missing costs are the ones that dominate real upgrades.** (Critic 3)
Regression testing, a production-clone rehearsal environment, report validation, mobile revalidation, training and comms, cutover window as a function of database size, and support posture are absent from a table headed "What will this actually cost you in engineering time?" Every one of them outweighs several items that are in the table. This is the largest content gap in the post, and fixing it requires no new research — the omissions are all things the authors already know.

**2. The thesis sentence is unsupported and is contradicted by the post's own tables.** (Critic 1)
*"For most organisations asking that question, the anxiety is misdirected"* has no population evidence behind it, and the modal reader — Manage-only, x86, current on S&S, coming from 9.1 — clears all five CAN gates and lands on the post's own **QUALIFIED AND URGENT** verdict with a **High** value delta. Either scope the claim to the segments the post actually demonstrates are blocked, or support it. As written, the most-quoted sentence in the post is the least defended.

**3. Identity is named the most likely upgrade-breaker and given no gate, while the two posts rank it inconsistently.** (Critic 3)
The FOMO post's closing calls identity consolidation *"the item most likely to break your upgrade."* The companion post calls it *"the one with the largest number of affected humans if it goes wrong"* — and ranks it fourth. Both cannot be right. Lived experience says identity is the top hazard, and it is the one with no gate to stop you when it is subtly wrong. Reconcile to one ranking across the series and give identity a cost row.

**4. Gate 4 quotes IBM's "no longer available" to mean "not yet shipped."** (Critic 1)
IBM's sentence plainly addresses withdrawn products. The post applies it to parity lag, producing *"Your options are to wait, or to lose the capability"* — its most alarming line — from a source that does not say that. The post's own next paragraph (*"your 9.2 date is set by your industry solution's ship date"*) describes waiting, not deleting. Split the two conditions. Note the scope of this fix carefully: the critic overreaches in claiming the conclusion collapses. IBM still provides no "wait inside the upgrade" mechanism, and Gate 4 remains the right gate to check first. What needs correcting is one over-extended quotation, not the gate.

**5. The SHOULD half has no numbers, and the cost of waiting omits risk economics.** (Critic 2)
A "Rough weight" column containing task descriptions is not a weight. And Gate 8 prices only lifecycle dates while ignoring the actual cost of running unsupported software: no patch path for a critical CVE, audit findings, cyber-insurance exposure. Two fixes, one section each: add ranges with stated assumptions (wide and caveated is fine), and add the incident/audit/insurance dimension to Gate 8. The second is the higher-value of the pair and the cheaper to write.

**6. "QUALIFIED BUT POINTLESS" absolves deferral of costs the series itself documents.** (Critics 1 and 3, converging from opposite directions)
*"A perfectly respectable place to be"* sits in the same series as *"the two-version jump is real for anyone still on 9.0"* and an n-1 policy under which one skipped version becomes two outage windows. Add compounding version debt, and the softer costs Critic 3 names — knowledge decay, inventory rot, environment drift, a regression surface that grows while you wait. Keep the verdict; remove the absolution. Also worth the ten seconds it costs: state that verdicts compose to the worst case, since real estates match three rows at once.

### Critics defending their own corner — do not change the post for these

**Critic 1 on the agentic AI evidence standard.** Requiring documentation before a business case is exactly correct for a buyer-side publication, and this is the field architect protecting a vendor's announcement from an ordinary standard of proof. *One narrow sub-point survives and is worth a clause:* the post could not read the Feature Channel readmes, so "exists only at announcement level" should read "not documented in the sources we could access." Same caution, honest epistemics.

**Critic 1 on self-serving positioning.** The weakest attack in the set. A consultancy that tells a large fraction of its readers to do nothing is arguing against its own revenue. A one-line commercial disclosure is worth adding on general principle, but the accusation does not land and the post should not soften its scepticism in response to it.

**Critic 1 on "things you don't control."** Technically correct — Gates 1 and 3 are self-remediable by the post's own text — but this is a critic scoring a debating point on a column header. The underlying distinction between admissibility and economics is sound and worth keeping. Fix the column, not the framework.

**Critic 2 on business drivers belonging in the gates.** Half corner-defending. A board AI mandate does not make an industry solution ship, and the post's central discipline — motivation does not create admissibility — is the most valuable thing it does. The fair residue is narrower than the CIO states: external drivers belong in the **SHOULD** half only, where they legitimately change the discount rate on waiting and fund the remediation that clears Gates 1 and 3. Do not let them into the CAN half.

**Critic 2 on "your blocker isn't the features" excusing underperformance.** This is a CIO relocating an accountability conversation into a blog post's scope. The post is describing a state of the world; it is not its job to run performance management. The adjacent suggestion — name an owner for preventing each gate recurring — is a genuine improvement, but it is a different critique wearing this one's clothes.

**Critic 3 on tone.** Substantially survivorship bias: they upgraded, it worked out, so deferral now looks like the wrong answer for everyone. For a customer genuinely blocked at Gate 4, "stop worrying" is correct and kind advice. The fair part of this critique is not the tone — it is the missing cost of deferral, already captured as fair item 6. Keep the honest verdicts and the permission to not upgrade.

**Critic 3 on Db2 not being #1.** Genuinely fair as a *ranking-method* criticism (rank by residual risk, not raw blast radius) and it is listed above in spirit under item 3. But the critic under-weights that their own competent DBA is the control making it look easy — for an estate without one, or with a procurement function that takes eight weeks, the item earns its placement. Reframe the ranking rationale; do not demote the item.

---

**Bottom line.** The post's method — sequential gates, verified sources, an explicit unverified table — is sound and unusually honest, and none of these three critics dented it. What they dented is the *content inside* the framework: an unsupported "most," an economics half with no economics, a cost table built from IBM's changelog rather than from real upgrade budgets, and a hazard ranking the post itself half-disagrees with. Fix items 1 through 3 and this becomes the best decision post in the series. Leave them and it remains a very well-sourced essay about the risks IBM already documented.
