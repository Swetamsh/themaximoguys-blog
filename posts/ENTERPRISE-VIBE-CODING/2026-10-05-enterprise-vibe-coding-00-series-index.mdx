# Enterprise Vibe Coding: Series Index

> 🎯 **Who this is for:** Developers, team leads and executives in regulated industries who keep hearing "vibe coding" and want to know what it looks like when the stakes are payroll, power grids and policyholder data.

**Series:** Part 0 of 13 — *Enterprise Vibe Coding* | **Read time:** 5 minutes

---

Picture two developers on the same Saturday.

The first is at her kitchen table, building a recipe app for fun. She types "make it look nicer and add a shopping list," the AI rewrites half the code, and she clicks accept without reading a line. If it breaks, she shrugs and asks again.

The second works at a regional utility. On Monday she has to change how outage credits are calculated for 400,000 customers. She would love the same speed. But her change needs a ticket, a reviewer, a test plan, and an auditor who can later ask, "Who changed this, and why?"

Both of them are vibe coding. Only one of them can afford to forget the code exists. This series is about the second developer.

---

## 🎯 Why This Series Exists

In early 2025, "vibe coding" was a joke-adjacent term for letting the AI drive. By 2026 it was a product category with enterprise price lists. GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code and Kiro all grew agent modes. IBM shipped Bob, which it pitches as an AI development partner built for enterprise teams, added European and Japanese regions in July 2026, and says an on-premises option for data-residency and regulated environments is on its roadmap.

Even the vendors can't agree on the name. IBM's own product messaging avoids "vibe coding" entirely, while the press happily calls Bob a vibe coding tool. IBM's explainers treat vibe coding as the informal end of a spectrum that runs through agentic coding to agentic engineering. Wherever you land on the label, the work is the same: AI writing a lot of the code, and humans staying accountable for it.

The tools changed faster than the playbooks. Most writing about vibe coding is either breathless ("10x everything!") or dismissive ("toy for demos"). Enterprise teams need something in between: what actually works, what the guardrails look like, and which numbers to trust.

So that is what we wrote: short posts, plain English, real examples, and one honest skeptical note in every part.

---

## 🧭 How to Read It

Each part stands alone and takes five to eight minutes. They build loosely in four arcs:

- **The shift (1–3):** what changed, prompts as specs, autocomplete to agents.
- **The guardrails (4–5, 9, 11):** planning, human approval, receipts, house rules.
- **The enterprise realities (6–8, 10):** legacy code, firewalls, model choice, honest numbers.
- **The Maximo finale (12–13):** what it means for EAM teams, plus your first Maximo AI agent.

## 🧰 The Tools You Will Meet

We do not crown a winner. Each tool shows a different enterprise answer:

- **GitHub Copilot** — the tool that put AI in most developers' editors first.
- **Cursor** — the AI-native editor many vibe coders started with.
- **Claude Code** — an agent that lives in your terminal and works across a whole repository.
- **Kiro** — Amazon's spec-driven editor that turns a prompt into requirements, design and tasks before it writes code.
- **IBM Bob** — IBM's enterprise-focused development partner with Plan, Ask and Agent modes, approval gates and admin analytics.

Independent analysts have been cautious, and we will be too. IDC noted at Bob's launch that the "external evidence base remains limited." That is fair for the whole category.

**Where to start:** new to the topic, read in order. Leading a team or budget, try Parts 1, 5, 7 and 10. Working with Maximo, read Part 3, then jump to 12 and 13.
