# Piloting Bob on Maximo: Metrics, Competitors, and the Open Questions

<aside>

🎯 **Who this is for:** Maximo leads, delivery managers, architects, and the person who has to defend an AI tooling budget in front of finance. You have read the Bob pitch and the 69% headline. You now need to decide what to do on Monday: buy, wait, or run an experiment, and how to make the experiment prove something.

</aside>

## 🔥 The Pilot That Proved Exactly What It Was Designed to Prove

Here is a conversation we have watched happen, in different clothes, at more than one shop.

*"The pilot went great,"* says the sponsor. *"Everyone loved it."*
*"How many tasks did it complete?"* — *"A lot. The team said it felt twice as fast."*
*"Who reviewed the output?"* — *"The same two seniors, in the evenings."*
*"How many of those changes broke something later?"* — *"We didn't track that."*
*"What did it cost per task?"* — *"We don't know yet. The invoice isn't in."*

Nothing in that exchange is dishonest. Everyone is sincere. And none of it is evidence. It is a **vibe report** about a tool whose whole marketing position is that it is *not* vibe coding.

IBM's own executives make that distinction on stage. Neel Sundaresan, who runs Automation and AI at IBM, said: "It's not about vibe coding; it's about literal programming. It's not about accessibility, it's about security." IBM Think content goes further and says vibe coding "caused a new type of technical debt called security debt." If the vendor holds that line, the least a customer can do is hold the same line in the pilot.

In [Part 1](/blog/mas-bob-01-what-is-ibm-bob) we described what Bob is. In [Part 2](/blog/mas-bob-02-models-security-self-hosted) we covered models, security, and the self-hosted option. In [Part 3](/blog/mas-bob-03-governance-pricing-bobcoins) we locked down the controls, the audit trail, and the economics. In [Part 4](/blog/mas-bob-04-bob-and-maximo) we walked through the four doors between Bob and Maximo. This final part answers the question the other four set up: **how do you find out, with your own data, whether Bob is worth having on your Maximo estate?**

<aside>

💡 **Key insight:** The goal of a pilot is not to feel good about a tool. It is to produce a number you would be comfortable defending to the person who funds it, *and* a list of things you still do not know. This part gives you both.

</aside>

## 📊 The Numbers IBM Publishes, and What Each One Is

Start by laying every figure on the table. The rule for this section comes straight from the field guide: **every figure here is IBM-reported or appears in an IBM-published customer story, and none has been independently audited.** The Register described IBM as "the latest to lean on its own staff to 'prove'" AI efficacy, and StockTitan noted that the productivity metrics are self-reported.

### How to read a case study, using Blue Pearl as the teaching example

The Blue Pearl story is a gift for training your skepticism, because it appears twice with different shapes. One IBM release says a typical 30-day Java upgrade took three days, saving over 160 engineering hours. A later release describes the same work as nine months with 14 engineers compressed into three days. Both cannot be the *baseline*. One is a per-upgrade number, the other is a program number, and nobody tells you which units the "three days" are in.

That is not an accusation. It is what marketing material looks like when several people write several releases. But it demonstrates the five questions you should ask of any vendor result, including ours:

| Question | Why it matters | Blue Pearl example |
|---|---|---|
| What was the baseline, and who measured it? | An estimate of "normally" is not a control | "Typical 30-day upgrade" vs "nine months, 14 engineers" |
| What was counted in the "after"? | Review, test, and rework are the expensive part | Not stated |
| Who did the work? | Experts on a known codebase flatter any tool | Customer engineers, unspecified |
| What was the unit? | Per task, per project, per year are different claims | Both appear |
| What did it cost to get there? | Coins, licenses, and senior review time | Not stated |

### The Maximo number, one more time

The IBM Maximo team figure deserves a short repeat because it travels furthest. IBM says the Maximo development team used Bob for code generation, refactoring, and updates that "normally take days" and finished them in hours, for "an estimated 69% time savings." As we showed in [Part 4](/blog/mas-bob-04-bob-and-maximo), that is **unusable for planning**: no task mix, no baseline, no sample, no method, and the team that did the work is the team that owns the codebase.

IBM's own Think writing helps you here. Adam McDaniel's pieces say "Token consumption is a cost signal, not a value metric," "AI doesn't fix a team. It amplifies what's already there," and "Review capacity becomes the limiting factor." Quote IBM to IBM. It is the politest way to ask for a pilot.

### The statistics IBM borrows from others

IBM's content also leans on third-party numbers. They are worth knowing because your stakeholders will have seen them.

| Statistic | Origin | Label | Use with care because |
|---|---|---|---|
| 85% of DevSecOps professionals say AI moved the bottleneck from writing to reviewing code | GitLab 2026 AI Accountability Report, cited in IBM's Jul 9 release | Third-party, repeated by IBM | It is a perception survey |
| Developers 19% slower while believing they were 20% faster | METR study | Third-party, repeated by IBM | Small sample, early-2025 tools (see below) |
| 84% use or plan to use AI; 46% distrust accuracy vs 33% trust | Stack Overflow 2025 survey | Third-party, repeated by IBM | Survey of attitudes, not outcomes |
| 79% of executives see AI productivity gains; only 29% can confidently measure ROI | IBM IBV | IBM research | That 29% is the gap this pilot closes |
| 60 to 80% of development budgets go to modernization | IBM Apr 28 release, external footnote | Cited by IBM | Footnoted source, not verified here |
| About 60% of work is migration, modernization, and maintenance; new code about 15% | Sundaresan, Think 2026 recap | IBM exec | Directionally matches Maximo reality, which is why this matters to you |

That last pair is the interesting one for a Maximo shop. If 60% of enterprise software work is migration, modernization, and maintenance, then the work Bob targets looks a lot like a Maximo estate: upgrade analysis, Java-to-script conversion, integration upkeep, report fixes. The *fit* argument is real. It is the *magnitude* argument that is unproven.

Note that the IDC paper is published through IBM, which is common for analyst work and worth keeping in mind.

### The six recurring criticisms, and a pilot test for each

Independent coverage keeps returning to the same complaints. A good pilot converts each complaint into something you can observe.

| # | Recurring criticism [IND] | What to observe in your pilot |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Productivity numbers are self-reported | Use timestamps, not surveys, and compare the two |
| 2 | Model choice is a black box | Log the date, time, and task for every response you rate; ask IBM which model family handled it |
| 3 | No OS-level sandbox; commands run as child processes | Use a disposable dev container and keep secrets out of the workspace (Part 3 covers `.bobignore` and workspace trust) |
| 4 | Legacy-language output can be dated or confidently wrong | Seed review with known pitfalls (for Maximo: deprecated Java APIs, `count()` in scripts, unclosed MboSets) and see whether Bob flags or repeats them |
| 5 | Air-gapped mode runs on a weaker model tier | If you will self-host, run the pilot on the self-hosted model, not the SaaS demo |
| 6 | Admin audit covers identity and admin events more than agent actions | Try to reconstruct, from logs alone, what the agent did to one specific change |

### The METR study, with its own caveat attached

IBM repeats the METR finding, so you will meet it. Here is what it was. METR ran a randomized controlled trial with **16 experienced open-source developers** working on **246 real issues** in mature repositories, averaging about two hours each. Developers forecast that AI would speed them up by 24%. With AI tools allowed, they took **19% longer**. After the fact, they still believed AI had sped them up by **20%**. Time was self-reported by the developers while their screens were recorded.

The authors were careful about what they did *not* claim: not that AI fails to help most developers, not that results apply beyond that setting, not that future AI won't improve, and not that other usage approaches couldn't produce speedups. They flagged learning effects (developers had about 50 hours of Cursor experience) and possible sampling bias. And the METR page now carries a prominent banner that these results are out of date, pointing to a February 2026 update on late-2025 tools.

So do not use METR as a stick to beat Bob with, and do not let anyone use "AI makes you faster" as a counterweight either. Use it for the one thing it proves beyond argument: **people are bad at estimating their own AI-assisted speed.** That is why the pilot in this part measures with timestamps and treats surveys as a separate, labeled column.

<aside>

💡 **Key insight:** The perception gap is the whole case for measurement. If developers who just did the work cannot tell whether they were 19% slower or 20% faster, your sponsor, who only saw the demo, certainly cannot.

</aside>

## ⚔️ The Competitive Landscape, Through a Maximo Lens

Bob does not arrive in an empty field. The field guide's landscape table is a fair starting point, and we extend it with the questions a Maximo team should ask.

### Positioning table

| Tool | Positioning [IBM/IND] | Versus Bob |

### The price context

| Tool | Entry or team price (2026; verify on vendor pages) |
|---|---|
| Bob Pro / Pro+ / Ultra | $20 / $60 / $200 per month (50 / 180 / 1,000 Bobcoins) |
| Cursor | $20 / $40 |

Bob's base price sits in the market band. The differences are the opaque coin-to-token rate, no model choice, and indemnity only at Enterprise. Part 3 covers the Bobcoin economics in detail.

### What matters for Maximo specifically

Most of the market comparison is about general coding. For Maximo, five questions decide more than the feature matrix.

| Maximo-specific question | Why it matters | How to test |
|---|---|---|
| Can the agent act as an MCP client against the MAS 9.2 server or Max_mcp? | Door 4 from Part 4 is the only door that touches live data | Read-only key, sandbox, `tools/list`, one safe read |
| Can you load house rules (autoscript standards, naming, logging) into the agent? | Output quality tracks context quality | Same task with and without a rules file |
| Does the agent handle Jython and Nashorn script patterns, `MboSet` handling, and launch point selection? | IBM's own community guidance says avoid `count()`, avoid `service.log()`, and don't call `save()` or `close()` on MboSets you did not get from MXServer | Seed a script with these anti-patterns and check findings |
| Can approvals and policies be enforced centrally? | Auto-approve is where the PromptArmor incident lived | Apply the Part 3 policy list, then try to bypass it |
| Is the cost per accepted task predictable? | Coins are consumed by failed attempts too | The ledger in this part |

Notice that the last four are agent-neutral. That is deliberate. **Run the same ledger against a second agent on a subset of tasks.** If Bob wins, you know it; if it ties, you have negotiating leverage; and if it loses, you saved yourself a year of licenses. TMG's Max_Interfaces, Max_mcp, and Max_autoscripts are agent-neutral by design, so the Maximo-side setup does not change when the agent does.

### A different category worth naming

One more thing for the landscape. Interloc Solutions has launched interloc.ai, which it describes as an agentic AI platform for IBM Maximo: an orchestrator coordinating specialized agents that interpret service requests, create and update work orders, and push approved actions through Maximo's existing APIs and security model. That is not a coding agent. It is an *operations* agent. We mention it so you do not conflate the categories: Bob helps people who build and maintain Maximo configuration and code, while operational agents act on the work management process itself. Different budgets, different risk owners, different pilots.

## 🧭 Eight Points of Guidance for Maximo Teams

The field guide ends its analysis with eight points. We expand each one into something you can assign to an owner and check off.

### The eight points, expanded

| # | Guidance | Maximo meaning | Owner | Evidence you can show |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Credible option, but don't buy it for the 69%** | Justify the pilot with fit (IBM skills, MCP, Local Dev Mode), not with the internal estimate | Sponsor | Business case cites your pilot metric, with the IBM figure footnoted |
| 2 | **Plan, then act** | Use Plan mode for any Manage customization and attach the plan to the change record | Change manager | Plan output linked in each change ticket |
| 3 | **Agents go through the API layer, never the database** | MAS 9.2 MCP server or Max_mcp, dedicated read-only API key first, scoped write key per use case, destructive tools annotated | Security / integration lead | Key inventory, group definition, annotation list |
| 4 | **Lock auto-approve centrally** | `DisabledAutoApprovalGroups=edit,execute,mcp` on machines touching client code; workspace trust; `.bobignore` for `.env`, keystores, `maximo.properties` | Endpoint / security | Policy file, screenshot of enforced state |
| 5 | **Feed it house rules** | Put autoscript standards into `.bob/rules/` or an `AGENTS.md` | Tech lead | Rules file in the repo, with a before/after task |
| 6 | **Choose SaaS vs self-hosted deliberately** | Regulated utilities, defense, and public sector may need self-hosted: OpenShift 4.20+, about 36.5 vCPU and 53 GiB for Bob Core, plus GPUs, no Bobalytics, weaker model tier | Architect / compliance | Decision record with the constraints that forced it |
| 7 | **Consultancies: watch the indemnity line** | Only Enterprise seats carry IP indemnity; Pro, Pro+, and Ultra seats on client code do not | Practice lead / legal | Seat type per engagement |
| 8 | **Measure value, not just activity** | Pair Bobalytics' Bob factor with defect escape rate, review hours, and Bobcoins per completed task | PMO | The scorecard in this part |

### Guidance 3 in practice: the key and the group

Because point 3 is where most pilots go wrong, here is the shape of a conservative setup. The details come from the pattern in [Part 4](/blog/mas-bob-04-bob-and-maximo), and every name below is a placeholder for your own.

```
# Pilot identity (non-production only)
Service user:        BOBPILOT_RO            # one human owner on record
Security group:      BOBPILOT_RO_GRP        # read-only on 2-3 object structures
API key label:       bob-pilot-readonly-w1  # rotate weekly; store in secret manager
MCP annotations:     all create/update/delete tools flagged destructive
alwaysAllow:         []                     # empty until weeks of observation
```

The point is that the **security group is the enforcement**. Approval clicks and annotations are gates and hints, not walls. IBM's documentation notes that when you refresh authentication tokens for API keys, new tokens are generated and the current ones expire, so plan rotation and expect integrations that cached the old token to fail until updated.

### Guidance 4 in practice: policy as a checklist

| Control | Setting | Verify by |
|---|---|---|
| Auto-approval disabled for risky groups | `DisabledAutoApprovalGroups=edit,execute,mcp` | Try to enable auto-approve in the UI; confirm it is blocked |
| Workspace trust | Enabled | Open an untrusted folder; confirm restricted behavior |
| Secret exclusion | `.bobignore` lists `.env`, keystores, `maximo.properties` | Ask Bob to read each file; confirm refusal |
| Update mode | `UpdateMode` set to a pinned value | Check the installed version on three machines |
| Hooks | `EnforcedHooks` logging every tool call | Reconstruct one change from the hook log |
| Bob Shell on CI | Policy coverage **unconfirmed** (documented as Bob IDE only) | Do not run Bob Shell in CI during the pilot |

That last row is an open question, and it appears again in the checklist at the end.

## 🎯 Designing the Pilot

This is the section to keep. Everything before it is context. Everything after it is what you do with the results.

### Principles

| Principle | Reason |
|---|---|
| **Matched tasks, not volunteers' favorites** | Cherry-picked tasks guarantee a good story |
| **Both arms timed with the same clock** | Self-report is the exact failure METR documented |
| **Count accepted work, not attempted work** | Failed attempts burn coins and reviewer time |
| **Review is a first-class cost** | IBM's own sources say it is the limiting factor |
| **Pre-register the stop conditions** | You will be tempted to explain away a bad week |
| **Record what you do not know** | The open-questions list is part of the deliverable |

### Scope: six weeks, 20 to 30 tasks, three task families

Pick task families that map to the high-fit rows from Part 4 and that you can test. Avoid anything that touches production data.

| Family | Example tasks | Why it suits a pilot | Verification |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Java MBO to automation script** | Convert a custom field-validation class to a script on an object launch point | Pattern-heavy and testable; IBM skill exists | Unit-style test in a sandbox; behavior diff |
| **B. Autoscript review and refactor** | Review existing scripts against your standards; fix `count()` use, logging, MboSet handling | IBM skill exists; house rules apply | Review checklist; performance measure on a copy |
| **C. Integration or report change** | Add a field to an outbound integration payload; adjust a BIRT report | Medium-high fit; needs accurate API context | Payload diff; report output compare |

Eight to ten tasks per family gives 24 to 30 in total. If you can only find twelve tasks, run twelve and report a wide uncertainty. Do not pad.

### Arms and assignment

For each task, define the **baseline method** (what your team would normally do) and the **Bob method**. For a small pilot, a pure randomized design is unrealistic, so use matched pairs:

1. Sort candidate tasks by estimated size (S, M, L) within each family.
2. Pair tasks of similar size and risk.
3. Flip a coin per pair to assign Bob or baseline.
4. Have developers of mixed seniority on both arms. Do not give Bob only to your best people.

If your team is too small to split, use a **crossover**: each developer does comparable tasks in both arms, in alternating order, to cancel learning effects. Learning effects are real. METR flagged them, and Bob has its own idioms such as Plan mode and rules files that take a couple of weeks to use well. Plan a week-one warm-up that is **excluded** from the numbers.

### The six-week schedule

| Week | Focus | Gate to continue |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Policy applied, ledger created, rules file written, read-only key issued; baseline tasks selected | Policy verified (checklist above) |
| 1 | Warm-up tasks, excluded from analysis | No security incidents |
| 2 to 3 | Family A and B tasks, both arms | Ledger complete for every task |
| 4 | Family C tasks; first review-hour read-out | Review hours per change not exploding |
| 5 | Remaining tasks; 30-day defect window starts for early merges | Defects logged consistently |
| 6 | Close ledger, compute metrics, write the go/no-go memo | Open-questions checklist updated |

Because defects escape *after* merge, six weeks is a floor. Plan a short follow-up read at day 30 and day 60 after the last merge, and state in the memo that defect escape numbers are provisional until then.

## 📏 The Metrics, Defined So They Cannot Be Fudged

Vague metrics are how pilots prove whatever the sponsor hoped for. Each metric below has a definition, a formula, a source of truth, and a trap.

### Headline metrics

| Metric | Definition | Formula | Source of truth | Trap to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Defect escape rate (DER)** | Share of merged changes that cause a defect after review, within a fixed window | defects attributed to merged changes ÷ merged changes, per arm, over 30 days | Your defect or incident tracker, tagged to the change ID | Counting only defects Bob "caused" versus defects in AI-assisted changes |
| **Review hours per accepted change** | Reviewer time from assignment to approval, including re-reviews after rework | sum of reviewer hours ÷ accepted changes | Timestamps in the pull request or change tool; reviewers log minutes | Reviewers skimming because the output looks polished |
| **Bobcoins per accepted task** | All coins consumed on the task, including failed and abandoned attempts, per task that was accepted | coins consumed ÷ accepted tasks | Bob usage data per user, tagged by task window | Dividing by *attempted* tasks, which flatters the tool |

### Supporting metrics

| Metric | Definition | Why it earns its place |
|---|---|---|
| **Acceptance rate** | Accepted tasks ÷ attempted tasks | Exposes tasks the agent could not finish |
| **Rework cycles** | Number of review-then-change loops before acceptance | Review capacity is the bottleneck |
| **Time to accepted** | Wall-clock from task start to acceptance | Captures waiting on reviewers |
| **Total human hours per accepted task** | Author + review + test + rework hours | The honest "time saved" figure |
| **Perception gap** | Developer's estimated time saved minus measured time saved | Quantifies the METR effect in your team |
| **Policy exceptions** | Count of blocked or overridden approvals | A security signal, not a productivity one |
| **Standards findings** | Count of violations of your autoscript standards in delivered output | Tests the "feed it house rules" point |

### Cost per accepted task, with an illustrative calculation

You will need a unit cost. Bobcoin pricing is published as $20 per month for 50 coins on Pro ($0.40 per coin), $60 for 180 on Pro+ (about $0.33), $200 for 1,000 on Ultra ($0.20), with Enterprise priced as custom allotments: standard packs at $500 per 1,000 coins and overage packs at $550 per 1,000, per IBM's Bobcoins page (DOC16 lists $550 for all packs; confirm with an IBM quote). IBM does not publish tokens per Bobcoin, and self-hosted pricing is not published. Individual overage was recorded at about $0.50 per coin by a third-party tracker. Verify all of these on bob.ibm.com before you use them.

Here is an **illustration with invented numbers**, to show the arithmetic. These are not measurements.

| Item | Bob arm | Baseline arm |
|---|---|---|
| Tasks attempted | 12 | 12 |
| Tasks accepted | 10 | 12 |
| Coins consumed (all attempts) | 60 | n/a |
| Coin price assumed | $0.55 (Enterprise pack rate) | n/a |
| Coin cost | $33.00 | $0 |
| Author hours (total) | 18 | 40 |
| Review hours (total) | 14 | 10 |
| Test and rework hours (total) | 9 | 8 |
| Human hours per accepted task | (18+14+9) ÷ 10 = **4.1** | (40+10+8) ÷ 12 = **4.8** |
| Coins per accepted task | 60 ÷ 10 = **6.0** | n/a |
| Human cost at $90/hour per accepted task | $369 | $432 |
| Coin cost per accepted task | $3.30 | $0 |
| **Total cost per accepted task** | **$372.30** | **$432.00** |

Read what the illustration says. Author hours fell sharply, review hours *rose*, and total human hours fell only a little. The coin cost is trivial next to the labor, which is typical: the bill that matters is people. A vendor figure that says "69% faster" and a measured figure that says "15% fewer total hours with more review burden" tell very different stories, and only the second helps you staff next quarter. If your real numbers look like this, the decision depends on defect escapes, and that is why DER is the first headline metric.

### The ledger

Create the ledger before week one and make it a rule that **no task starts without a row**. A flat file is enough. Here is a CSV header and one example row.

```
task_id,family,size,arm,developer,started_at,accepted_at,author_min,review_min,test_min,rework_cycles,coins_used,accepted,change_id,defects_30d,standards_findings,dev_estimate_saved_min,notes
A-03,A,M,bob,dev2,2026-10-20T09:05,2026-10-20T14:40,95,70,40,1,6,yes,CHG-10482,0,1,120,"converted validation MBO; Plan output attached"
```

And a short script to compute the headline metrics per arm. It is deliberately plain so reviewers can audit it.

```python
import csv
from collections import defaultdict

rows = list(csv.DictReader(open("ledger.csv")))
arms = defaultdict(lambda: dict(n=0, acc=0, coins=0, review=0, human=0, defects=0))

for r in rows:
    a = arms[r["arm"]]
    a["n"] += 1
    a["coins"] += int(r["coins_used"] or 0)        # failed attempts included
    if r["accepted"] == "yes":
        a["acc"] += 1
        a["review"] += int(r["review_min"])
        a["human"] += int(r["author_min"]) + int(r["review_min"]) + int(r["test_min"])
        a["defects"] += int(r["defects_30d"] or 0)

for arm, a in arms.items():
    acc = max(a["acc"], 1)
    print(f"{arm}: accept={a['acc']}/{a['n']}  "
          f"review_h/accepted={a['review']/60/acc:.2f}  "
          f"human_h/accepted={a['human']/60/acc:.2f}  "
          f"coins/accepted={a['coins']/acc:.1f}  "
          f"DER={a['defects']/acc:.2f}")
```

Two details matter. First, `coins_used` is summed for every row, accepted or not, so abandoned attempts raise the numerator. Second, human hours include review and test, so a change that is fast to write and slow to trust gets no free pass.

### Capturing Bobcoins per task

Bob's usage data is reported per user, not per task, so you need a convention to attribute coins. A simple one works: each developer records the Bobcoin balance before and after each task window, and the project lead reconciles weekly against the account usage view. Where the tool shows usage in a different grain, reconcile the weekly total to the ledger sum and report any difference. If the totals disagree by more than about 10%, say so in the memo rather than quietly rounding.

## 🛑 Stop Conditions and Go/No-Go Thresholds

Write these down **before** week one and get the sponsor to initial them. People are far more flexible about stop conditions after the fact.

### Stop conditions (any one halts the pilot)

| Trigger | Action |
|---|---|
| An agent action touches a production system or production data | Stop immediately, revoke the key, run an incident review |
| A secret appears in a prompt, log, or output | Stop, rotate, review `.bobignore` and workspace contents |
| An approval policy is bypassed or disabled on any pilot machine | Pause until the cause is fixed |
| Defect escape rate on the Bob arm exceeds the baseline arm by more than an agreed margin after 4 weeks | Pause and review the failure patterns |
| Reviewer hours per accepted change grow week over week for three weeks | Pause and test whether the bottleneck is rules, task choice, or review practice |
| Any task is found to have fabricated a Maximo object, field, or API that review missed | Log as a critical finding and add to the review checklist |

### Go/no-go matrix

These thresholds are examples. Set yours with the sponsor, and set them before you see the data.

| Result | Reading | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| DER at or below baseline, total human hours down 15% or more, review hours not up more than 25% | Credible, review-sustainable benefit | **Go** for a wider rollout in the task families that won |
| DER at or below baseline, total human hours flat, review hours up | Work shifted, not saved | **Conditional**: improve rules and task selection, re-pilot four weeks |
| DER above baseline | Quality cost exceeds speed benefit | **No-go** until the failure patterns are fixed |
| High acceptance, low review confidence (reviewers say they cannot verify) | The tool is outrunning your review process | **No-go** for that task family; consider only with stronger test harness |
| Good results only in Family A | Fit is narrow | **Go narrowly**: license a few seats, keep the rest out |

Notice that a *narrow* go is a perfectly good outcome. Java-to-script conversion alone may justify a small number of seats for a defined project, and you do not owe anyone an estate-wide rollout because a pilot succeeded somewhere.

## 🔬 Worked Scenarios: What You Will Actually See

These are the situations our practitioners expect, based on the Part 4 scenarios and the pattern of independent reports. They are **forecasts of what to look for, not results**, because we have not run a customer pilot.

### Scenario 1: The conversion that passes the sniff test

A custom Java MBO validation class becomes an automation script. Bob's output compiles, runs, and passes the test you wrote. The reviewer approves in ten minutes.

What to check before you count this as a win:

| Check | Why |
|---|---|
| Is the **launch point** equivalent to where the Java ran? | A different event changes when the logic fires |
| Are `MboSet` handling and cleanup consistent with IBM's guidance? | IBM community guidance says not to call `save()` or `close()` on MboSets you did not get from MXServer |
| Does the script avoid `count()` where `notExist()` would do? | IBM community guidance flags `count()` for performance because it requires returning all records |
| Is logging object-specific and guarded by `isDebugEnabled`? | The same guidance recommends object-specific loggers over `service.log()` |
| Did anything about error messages or message keys change? | Users see these |

If the script passes all five, that task is accepted, counted, and its review time logged at its real value. If it passes the tests but fails two of the five, that is exactly the kind of finding that belongs in "standards findings" and in your house rules file.

### Scenario 2: The review that finds real problems and invented ones

You point the autoscript optimizer skill at fifteen existing scripts. It reports forty findings. Some are real, such as `count()` in a hot path. Some are stylistic. A couple refer to a method that does not exist on that object.

This is where the independent critiques bite. The IBM i issue that triggered the "confidently hallucinates" comment was a wrong claim about a real parameter value. Your control is simple: **every finding gets a verdict**.

| Verdict | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Confirmed | Reviewer reproduced or verified against IBM docs |
| Style only | Valid but not a defect; goes to standards, not defects |
| Wrong | Finding is inaccurate; log the claim and the correct answer |
| Unverifiable | Reviewer cannot tell; counts against review hours and is a warning sign |

Report the **wrong plus unverifiable share** as a headline in the memo. If one finding in five is wrong, your review cost is real and no vendor percentage reflects it.

### Scenario 3: The integration change that needs context

You ask for a new field in an outbound payload and a matching mapping change. Without an accurate API catalog in context, Bob guesses at an attribute name. With an object structure export and your standards file in context, it gets it right on the first pass. This is the Bordelanne conclusion in miniature: "The quality of the output depends heavily on the quality of the context provided."

The pilot test is cheap: run the same task twice, once with a thin prompt and once with rules and an API catalog, and log the coin cost and the review result of each. If context lifts acceptance and cuts review hours, that is evidence for investing in rules files regardless of which agent you buy. Max_Interfaces' endpoint library and Max_autoscripts' standards file are natural context sources, and both are MIT-licensed.

### Scenario 4: The tempting read-write experiment

Week five, someone proposes: "Let's let it fix the stuck work orders through MCP." This is the demo from [Part 4](/blog/mas-bob-04-bob-and-maximo) and it is the moment a pilot either keeps its discipline or loses it.

| Question | Required answer before proceeding |
|---|---|
| Is the key scoped to named object structures and a defined set of sites? | Yes, in writing |
| Is there a dry run that lists the records it would touch? | Yes, reviewed by a human |
| Are destructive tools annotated? | Yes |
| Is this a non-production environment? | Yes |
| Who approves, and where is that recorded? | A named person, in the change record |

If any answer is no, the experiment waits. A pilot designed to measure code-change productivity should not turn into a production automation project halfway through.

## ⚠️ Pitfalls and Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Developers report big savings, timers show little | The perception gap; self-reported time | Report both columns, trust the timers |
| Review hours climb while authoring hours fall | Work moved from writing to reviewing | Improve rules, add tests, shrink diff sizes; do not hide the shift |
| Bobcoin consumption is erratic across similar tasks | Unknown model routing; long contexts; retries | Record task context size and retries; ask IBM about routing |
| Coins per accepted task far above expectations | Failed attempts and abandoned sessions | Count them; they are real cost |
| Findings cannot be verified by reviewers | Output sounds authoritative but is not checkable | Require evidence (doc link, test) per finding |
| Pilot machines show different approval behavior | Policy not uniformly enforced | Re-verify the policy checklist; pin `UpdateMode` |
| Defects appear in the baseline arm too | Task difficulty, not tool | That is why you matched tasks; report relative, not absolute |
| Skill output differs from README promises | IBM moved paths or names | Pin the commit of every IBM asset, as Part 4 advised |
| Tool works great on SaaS, poorly self-hosted | Different model tier | Evaluate the deployment you will actually run |
| Sponsor wants the 69% on the slide | Anchoring | Offer the pilot number and keep IBM's figure in a labeled footnote |

### Five failure patterns we expect most

1. **The senior-only pilot.** Only your best developer uses Bob, so success cannot be separated from the person.
2. **The warm-up counted.** Week-one learning effects distort the first numbers in both directions.
3. **The unreviewed win.** Output that looks polished is approved quickly, and the defects arrive after the window.
4. **The moving goalposts.** Thresholds change after the data arrives.
5. **The scope creep.** A code pilot becomes a production-automation project.

1. **Pilot before you buy,** and buy only what the pilot supports.
2. **Never cite an IBM number without the word "IBM-reported."**

6. **Match your tasks** and include people of mixed seniority on both arms.
7. **Write the stop conditions first** and have the sponsor initial them.
8. **Keep agents on the API layer,** read-only first, with the security group as the wall.
9. **Test the deployment you will run,** especially if that is self-hosted.
10. **Publish your unknowns** alongside your results.
